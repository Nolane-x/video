# Cannae — visual bible

## Creative premise
The army that appears to be winning is already being surrounded. The film does not present Cannae as a magic trick or an immaculate chess game. It visualizes the causal interaction between the projecting Carthaginian centre, the Roman infantry's forward pressure, the Libyan infantry on both wings and Carthaginian cavalry superiority.

## Format and engine
- Long-form English history film; 16:9, 1920×1080, 24 fps.
- Primary renderer: H2 Squire 2D/vector engine from h2-squire-studio v1.2.2.
- No Remotion substitutions. Squire remains a separate renderer.
- No baked-in long subtitles; deliver SRT and VTT sidecars. Use short map labels and in-world signs only.
- VoiceStudio narration is one consistent English narrator, selected from its live manifest by exact ID and retained as a provenance bundle.

## Visual language
- Roman colour: muted brick / iron red (#9f4037).
- Carthaginian colour: blue-grey / indigo (#3e5e82).
- World: pale Apulian limestone, dry ochre ground, subdued olive scrub, river blue, parchment tactical maps.
- Contrast: slightly warm daylight for campaign and deployment; dusty high-key field for the opening; restrained dusk and desaturated shadows for the aftermath.
- No neon glows, generic gradients, HUD panels, photo-real AI humans or gore.

## Character continuity
- Use Squire's round-headed vector ancient presets as stylized stand-ins only: hastatus/princeps/triarius for Roman infantry types; latin/latinOld/velites for generic ancient opposing or skirmisher silhouettes.
- These presets are not authenticated Punic, Iberian, Gallic or Roman uniforms. Distinguish forces by palette, position, banner accent and labels, not by claiming a generic preset is historically exact.
- Hannibal, Varro and Paullus are never portrayed as photoreal likenesses. Use a generic commander close-up, map position and attributed name card only where needed.

## Visual rhythm
- Opening crush: close reaction, compressed human silhouettes, limited visibility.
- Campaign context: geographic map with Cannae, Canusium and the Aufidus / Ofanto river.
- Force comparison: one board comparison for infantry and cavalry; avoid repeating numbers in every shot.
- Deployment: overhead crescent centre, Libyan wings and cavalry wings.
- Mechanism: overhead blocks advance, curve folds backward, wings pivot inward, cavalry arcs toward rear.
- Human scale: close-ups, shield/spear/standard inserts and medium battlefield scenes.
- Aftermath: lower contrast, less movement, sober map and empty-field holds.
- Ending: strategic distinction between a tactical victory and the larger war.

## Motion and editing
- No zoom-only animation. Alternate map moves, overhead formations, character acting, prop inserts, split comparisons and establishing wides.
- Show anticipation → action → contact → follow-through → recovery on cavalry, shields and infantry.
- Avoid more than three consecutive shots using the same template.
- Tactical diagrams must match the line being narrated; no decorative battlefield diagrams.

## Sound arc
1. Low ancient drone, light wind, restrained drums under the hook.
2. Map/campaign: low pulse, sparse page/map SFX.
3. Deployment: measured march drums and a distant horn.
4. Initial clash: occasional hooves, armour, sword/shield impacts and distant battle sound.
5. Centre folds: tighten the pulse; avoid a wall of loud effects.
6. Envelopment: controlled battle roar, hooves and metal accents beneath clear narration.
7. Aftermath: remove percussion, use low somber bed and intentional space.
8. Conclusion: restrained mystery/ancient motif, not a triumphant victory cue.

## Historical and editorial boundaries
- Force counts are attributed to Polybius and presented as ancient estimates.
- Casualty totals are not declared exact; use "tens of thousands" and contextualize Polybius's much higher figure as an ancient estimate.
- The precise second-by-second choreography is a reconstruction. The film's formations are explanatory, not a measured reenactment.
- No fabricated quotations or dialogue.
- Respect victims; no gore or celebratory imagery of mass death.
