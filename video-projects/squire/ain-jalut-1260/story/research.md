# Research ledger — Ain Jalut, 1260

## Verified narrative facts and caveats
1. Baghdad was captured by Hülegü's Mongol army in February 1258; the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad ended. Medieval casualty totals vary and are not modern counts.
2. Hülegü continued west after Baghdad; Aleppo fell in early 1260 and Damascus submitted.
3. Möngke, Great Khan of the Mongol Empire, died in 1259. Hülegü withdrew east with much of his main army amid the succession crisis.
4. Kitbuqa commanded the Mongol force remaining in Syria. Exact strength estimates are uncertain; do not turn a historian's estimate into an exact muster.
5. Qutuz ruled the Mamluk Sultanate in Egypt in 1260 and chose to resist rather than submit.
6. Baybars played a major role in the Mamluk vanguard and later became sultan.
7. The Mamluk army moved north from Egypt in summer 1260; its march involved supply, intelligence, route and political considerations.
8. Acre remained outside the battle; the coastal political situation affected the movement and supply of the Mamluk army.
9. The battle was fought on 3 September 1260 at Ayn Jalut in the Jezreel Valley, in the region of modern Israel / Palestine.
10. The Mamluk victory halted the Mongol advance into Egypt and checked Mongol expansion in the southwest; it did not end Mongol power or all later campaigns.
11. Medieval accounts differ on the exact sequence of the battle, especially the role of a feigned retreat. One version is reported by Rashid al-Din; other accounts differ, and modern scholars question the neat, fully scripted version.
12. Qutuz was assassinated on the return journey after the battle; Baybars became sultan in October 1260. Do not assert a single certain motive for the assassination.
13. Mamluk cavalry used horses, bows, lances, swords, maces and armour; equipment varied by wealth and unit.
14. Mongol forces relied heavily on mounted archery and mobility, but should not be depicted as an identical mass of lightly armed horsemen.
15. Both sides were experienced mounted forces; this was not a simplistic battle of slow infantry against fast cavalry.
16. The political and strategic result outlasted the field engagement: Mamluk-Egyptian power became a durable regional counterweight to the Ilkhanids.

## Editorial policy
- Do not repeat sensational medieval casualty totals as fact.
- Do not claim the Mongols were permanently stopped.
- Do not state that a feigned retreat is definitively what won the battle.
- Do not use exact army sizes unless explicitly attributed as an estimate and qualified.
- Squire's generic character rigs are visual shorthand, not authenticated uniforms.
