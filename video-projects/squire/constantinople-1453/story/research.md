# Research ledger — Constantinople 1453

## Core date frame
- Siege begins: 6 April 1453.
- Final assault and fall: 29 May 1453.
- Elapsed campaign: commonly described as 53 days.
- The narrative uses this interval as the spine.

## Main claims to source
1. Strategic importance of the city and Bosphorus / Golden Horn geography.
2. Construction of Rumeli Hisarı in 1452 and Ottoman control pressure on the Bosphorus.
3. Ottoman artillery and very large bombards; Orban's role is associated with the siege, but exact details/attribution require caution.
4. Theodosian land walls and repeated bombardment/repair cycle.
5. Four Christian ships reaching the city on 20 April 1453.
6. Ottoman ships appearing in the Golden Horn after an overland portage operation, conventionally dated 22 April; route and details are debated.
7. Repeated assaults, mining/countermining and defender exhaustion.
8. Giustiniani wounded and withdrawn during the final assault; do not claim this single event alone caused the fall.
9. Constantine XI died during the fall according to the main tradition; exact circumstances remain uncertain.
10. Conquest brought violence and enslavement; city later became an Ottoman imperial capital and was rebuilt.

## Academic starting point
- Anthony Kaldellis, *1453: The Conquest and Tragedy of Constantinople*, Oxford University Press, 2026. Relevant chapter abstracts: “The Final Assault”, “The Defenses Hold” and “Mehmed's Plan of Attack”. Publisher pages describe the battered Mid-Wall, Giustiniani's wound/withdrawal, the sea relief convoy and the operation that brought Ottoman ships into the Golden Horn. The chapter descriptions themselves caution that narrative details and blame are contested.
- Philippides and Hanak, *The Siege and the Fall of Constantinople in 1453: Historiography, Topography, and Military Studies* (Ashgate, 2011), for a broad survey of sources and historiography.
- Nicolò Barbaro, *Diary of the Siege of Constantinople 1453* (primary eyewitness account, read alongside other partisan and retrospective sources).
- George Sphrantzes, *Chronicon Minus* (primary/near-contemporary Byzantine source; compare with other accounts).
- Kritoboulos of Imbros, *History of Mehmed the Conqueror* (Ottoman-side near-contemporary narrative).
- Doukas, *Decline and Fall of Byzantium to the Ottoman Turks* (near-contemporary narrative).
- Leonard of Chios, letter/report on the fall (Latin Christian account, read with its rhetorical context).

## Disputed / uncertainty notes
- The route, mechanics, and precise number of ships hauled into the Golden Horn differ among accounts and later retellings. Show the operation as a reported strategic manoeuvre, not a fully documented modern engineering blueprint.
- The role of a single breach, gate or sudden accident in the final collapse is not treated as settled.
- Giustiniani's wound and withdrawal are included as an event in the narrative tradition; causal claims about the defenders' morale are framed cautiously.
- The emperor's precise death scene is not recreated as fact. No last words are invented.
- Medieval population, casualty and army-size figures vary significantly; avoid unqualified precision.
- The composite narrator is a story device, not a historical source.

## Visual evidence policy
Paintings and later reconstructions may inform visual mood, not establish exact uniforms, flags, ships or tactics. Keep designs stylised and plausible, and label disputed visuals as reconstruction.
