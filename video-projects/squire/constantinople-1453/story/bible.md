# Story bible — 53 Days to the End of an Empire

## Logline
A city that has survived for centuries faces an enemy that refuses to solve the siege in only one way. Over fifty-three days, artillery, naval adaptation, engineering and relentless pressure turn the supposedly impregnable walls into a battle of exhaustion.

## Audience promise
Explain how the siege worked through people, geography, engineering and time—not a list of dates. Every historical fact should have a visible cause and consequence.

## Emotional spine
- Opening: dread before the final assault.
- First act: confidence in the walls, followed by the first artillery wounds.
- Second act: the city thinks the sea may save it, then watches the Golden Horn become threatened from within.
- Third act: exhausted defenders face a final assault in which small command failures and the physical state of the walls become decisive.
- Ending: an empire ends, while the city continues under a new political order.

## Point of view
The “you” in the narration is a fictional composite defender, not a named or documented witness. No invented quotations are attributed to Constantine XI, Mehmed II, Giustiniani or any historical source.

## Historical discipline
- Use 6 April–29 May 1453 as the 53-day siege frame.
- Describe Orban's role and the largest bombardments carefully; do not treat every giant-cannon story as certain.
- The exact route and operational details of the ships hauled into the Golden Horn are not presented as fully documented engineering facts.
- Giustiniani's wound and withdrawal are described, but their precise impact on the collapse is not over-simplified.
- The emperor's death is reported in the tradition, but the exact circumstances are explicitly left uncertain.
- The city’s conquest includes violence and enslavement; do not sanitise it.
- The Ottoman conquest does not erase the city's people, buildings or later history.

## Character continuity
- Composite defender: dark blue-grey tunic, mail/helmet when in combat, worn cloak, same shield and belt across wall sequences.
- Byzantine/Latin defenders: varied blue, muted red and weathered steel; distinguish by unit rather than a simplistic uniform.
- Ottoman forces: muted red, ochre, dark leather and steel; avoid modern national flags or anachronistic insignia.
- Mehmed: shown in command compositions, not given fabricated dialogue.
- Giustiniani: labelled only where required; no invented likeness claim.
