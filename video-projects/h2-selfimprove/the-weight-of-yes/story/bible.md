# THE WEIGHT OF YES — character, camera and art bible

## Core idea
A dependable young person says yes so quickly that everybody else becomes lighter while his own life gets heavier. The turning point is not a dramatic rejection of other people: it is one calm, clear boundary, followed by a believable response and a healthier exchange of care.

## Locked brief
- Target runtime: 143 seconds, 29 shots, 16:9, 1920×1080 at 24 fps.
- Language: English on-screen text; no voice-over.
- Engine: native H2 SelfImprove Studio Canvas/Chromium with FFmpeg audio mastering.
- Accent: amber is a small signal for care, agency and the end card; cooler night tones carry the cost of automatic agreement.
- Tone: intimate, cinematic, humane. No villainizing friends, no magical transformation, no generic motivational montage.
- Sound arc: soft morning ambience → accumulating requests and low tension → real silence at the choice → warm but restrained release.

## Character continuity
- Hero: same cream round-headed young man throughout. Maintain the same hoodie/outfit and body proportions; let posture tell the arc.
- Friends: use the existing friend/girl presets as ordinary classmates, never as caricatures. Their requests are plausible and small; their reactions to his boundary are human.
- Acting arc: quick eager reach at first; carrying posture becomes closed and strained; a long held beat before answering; calm upright posture for the first “no”; then relaxed, reciprocal interaction.
- No shadow monster or supernatural antagonist. The antagonist is a reflexive habit and the social assumption that he will always absorb the extra work.

## Visual grammar
- Establish a repeating ledger: favors rise while the hero's own work remains unfinished. Use small native HUD/insert details only when they clarify the story.
- Make “yes” physical: hands accept, shoulders fold, gaze drops toward what he is carrying.
- Keep the camera motivated. Use slow pushes on unnoticed overload; hold a wider frame at the boundary so the viewer can read both characters; don't cut away before the other person's response.
- Use readable, non-overlapping text. Avoid covering faces, hands or device UI.
- Use the classroom/studio for requests and shared labor; café for the cost of postponed rest and the reciprocal friendship; kitchen/bedroom for private recovery; city for carrying obligations through ordinary life; void only for the brief overload beat.
- Use transitions sparingly. No rapid generic montage to pretend there is character development.

## Critical continuity and editorial gates
1. The hero accepts help requests physically in early shots; the first half must not look like phone addiction.
2. His own deadline and task visibly remain unresolved while the favor count rises.
3. The midpoint overload beat is followed by a pause, not a triumphant instant transformation.
4. Show the other person's reaction to “No. Not today.” The friend accepts it without villain behavior.
5. Another person takes over the task, so the resolution is practical rather than magical.
6. The hero submits his own project; this is the concrete payoff.
7. Ending shows he still chooses to help sometimes and declines other requests calmly.
8. No voice-over. Text is sparse, timed and legible; music must not flatten the silent decision.
