# Historical reference: Orban's great bombard and the Theodosian defences

Prepared for the Constantinople 1453 3D proof. This is a reconstruction brief, not a claim that the surviving 1464 Dardanelles Gun is the exact 1453 weapon.

## Model facts from the 2019 technical reconstruction

- The paper treats Orban’s gun as a huge bronze bombard and proposes a two-part construction: a longer barrel and a shorter, thicker powder chamber, cast separately and assembled in situ after transport (pp. 482–483).
- Its model uses a granite stone ball about 752 mm in diameter and approximately 600 kg (p. 482). These are reconstructed/model values based on historical reports, not a surviving original projectile with a modern measurement record.
- The model places the gun about 500 m from the inner wall and considers an average firing position about 1.5 m above ground. It discusses a prepared position of compacted earth and gravel, a protective ditch, and heavy fixed supports to anchor the gun and absorb recoil (pp. 484–485).
- The paper's reconstructed scenario for 24 April 1453 places Giustiniani's force near the Mesoteichion / Saint Romanos sector. It describes the outer wall towers and parateichion as obstructing the view of lower sections of the inner wall, meaning the crew could only see and target an upper section (p. 485).
- The paper cites historical reports of only three to seven shots per day for the great gun, reflecting slow loading and the difficulty of identifying the correct elevation (p. 486). Do not animate this as rapid automatic fire.
- In the article's cited reconstruction, the first successful impact weakens/tilts the masonry and the second shot opens a substantial breach (pp. 486–487). This is one analytical reconstruction of contested historical accounts, not a certainty that every breach happened in exactly this way.

## Required changes before an artistic approval gate

1. Replace the generic wheeled field-carriage look with a massive timber bed/cradle, a prepared earth-and-gravel firing platform, lateral retaining timber and fixed recoil supports. If wheels are shown, depict them only as transport equipment, not as the gun's ordinary firing mount.
2. Model a two-section bronze gun with a visible join/collar and a distinctly thicker, shorter rear powder chamber. Use the 9.2 m overall length from the paper as a scale reference, while marking the geometry as an informed reconstruction rather than an exact recovered design.
3. Match the bore and projectile scale: a roughly 0.752 m stone sphere should sit correctly against the muzzle/bore; the current proof's oversized bore/undersized ball relationship needs correction.
4. Build layered defences: ditch/paratichion, outer wall with towers, and taller inner wall. The gun's sightline should be composed to show why the outer fortifications interfere with aiming at lower portions of the inner wall.
5. Give the bombard crew a visibly laborious loading cycle with many hands and tools, but avoid a modern breech-loader workflow or implausibly rapid firing.
6. Stage wall damage as a readable sequence: impact, dust/shock, masonry cracking/tilting, delayed secondary collapse or follow-up impact, then repair under fire. Use distinct camera distances and foreground/midground/background action instead of a repeated static wide shot.
7. Retain uncertainty labels: the 1464 Dardanelles Gun is a comparison object, not the exact Orban bombard; the 2019 engineering paper makes a model from historical reports and assumptions.

## Historical/technical QA notes

- Do not state that the 2019 study proves Orban personally died in the gun explosion; its introduction repeats a reported tradition and investigates possible failure mechanisms.
- The paper's abstract and its geometry table differ on a powder-charge estimate. Do not use a single exact powder-charge figure in voiceover or on-screen labels without reconciling the source.
- The current 39-second render is only a proof of one bombard sequence. Passing 240fps/1080p QA is a technical milestone, not a historical or artistic approval.
